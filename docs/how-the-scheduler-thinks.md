# How the schedule thinks

Every round robin this app builds keeps the same set of promises. When two of
them collide, the one higher on this list wins. That is the whole algorithm,
told in order.

## 1. Sit-outs are fair

Nobody sits out twice before everyone has sat out once. Who sits first is pure
luck, drawn fresh each session, so the same name is never stuck opening the
bench. Once everyone has had a turn, the rotation starts over in the exact
order it took the first time: if Joe sat in round 1 and Jill in round 2, the
next cycle is Joe again, then Jill. Nobody ever sits twice in a row.

Couples set up under Set Partners sit together or not at all. A latecomer joins
the back of the queue and does not push out someone still waiting to play.

## 2. New partners before repeat partners

You partner someone new every round until you have partnered everyone you can.
The schedule also remembers when each pairing happened, not just that it did,
so the same team is never sent back out two rounds running. In a long session
the numbers can force a second pairing. When that happens the schedule spreads
the repeats out and keeps them rare: with twelve players it typically uses all
sixty-six possible pairings before any third meeting.

The one planned exception is a special game type. Four women in a gendered
rotation will run out of fresh combinations, and that is fine. Those forced
pairings still count, so the same two are not teamed up again in an ordinary
round.

## 3. Play everyone at least once

Beyond partners, the schedule works toward every player sharing a court with
every other, as teammate or opponent. Facing the same person over and over is
penalised the same way repeat partnering is, just less severely.

## 4. Even games

Teams are matched by rating, aiming for a combined gap of half a point or less.
This matters, but variety matters more: rather than repeat a partnership, the
schedule will accept a slightly wider gap. In practice about 98 courts in 100
stay on target, and a truly lopsided game is still refused.

## 5. A fair shot at special game types

When Gendered, Mixed or Skill rounds are on, the roster does not always
stretch to every court. Whoever missed the format last time goes first when it
comes around again, and the same tracking decides who keeps a place on court
when the round has sit-outs.

## 6. Courts lean gendered or mixed

Most people would rather play an all-men, all-women or two-and-two game than
three-and-one, so the schedule nudges courts toward those shapes. One woman
with three men is avoided hardest. This is the lowest promise on the list. It
never costs anyone a fresh partner or an even game, and when the men playing
are odd in number, one 3:1 court is unavoidable that round.

## The court that is short

A roster that does not divide by four is no reason to bench anyone. The last
court plays whoever is left: three people play a 2v1 with the strongest alone,
two play singles. Turns on that court rotate exactly like sit-outs do, and the
pair side goes to two people who have not teamed up lately.

## Nothing forgets

All of this memory is rebuilt from the rounds themselves every time the
schedule changes. Reshuffle, reload the page, add a player at half past nine
or lose one to a pulled calf, and the rotation, the pairing history and who is
owed a mixed game all carry forward. Pressing Reshuffle never wipes a round
already played.

## What the host can override

A padlocked pair beats everything for that round. A special game type beats
Set Partners, but only for the couples it has to split, and only for that
round. Everything else is the schedule keeping its promises in the order
above.
