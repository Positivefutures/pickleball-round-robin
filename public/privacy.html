Privacy Policy

Pickleball Round Robin Generator

The short version

  • The app works with no account, and if you never make one, nothing you type ever leaves your device.
  • Making an account is optional. It exists for one reason: so your groups appear on your other phone or tablet.
  • If you do make one, the only thing you are asked for is an email address. There is no password.
  • Nothing is sold, rented, or handed to advertisers. There are no ads in the app.
  • You can delete your account, and everything saved with it, from inside the app. You do not have to ask anyone.
  • You can download a copy of everything the account holds, from the same screen.

Who is behind the app

The Pickleball Round Robin Generator is made and run by Jeff Baker, in Canada. It is one person, not a company. For anything on this page, write to jeff@pbroundrobin.com.

Using the app without an account

This is how most people use it, and it is the default. Your groups, your players, their ratings and the schedule you are running are saved in your own browser, on your own device. They are not sent anywhere. There is no copy of them for anyone to look at, including me.

That also means they are only as safe as the device. Clearing your browsing data, or losing the phone, loses them. Import / Export in the app makes a file you can keep, and an account makes a copy on the server. Those are the two ways to have a second copy.

If you make an account

An account copies four things to a server, so another device can pick them up:

  • Your email address, which is how you sign in and the only thing about you personally that is asked for.
  • Your groups, meaning their names.
  • Your players, meaning the name you typed, the rating you gave, and which groups they play in.
  • Your settings, such as how many courts and rounds you usually play, and the rating a new player starts at.

That is the whole list. The schedule you are running right now, which rounds are finished, and who has sat out are not part of it. They stay on the device that built them.

The rows are locked to your account by the database itself, so one account cannot read, change, or delete another account's groups and players. That is not a promise about how the app is written. It is a rule the database enforces on every single request, and it has been tested with two real accounts.

The names you type in are other people's

Worth saying plainly. If you run a group, you are typing your players' names into your own device, and if you have an account, up to the server as well. Usually that is a first name and a skill rating, which is about as little as a player list can be. Even so, they are your players' names and not yours. Tell them the list exists if that would matter to them, and remove anyone who asks. Deleting a player in the app removes them from the server too.

How signing in works

There is no password, and there never has been. You type your email address, a six digit code is emailed to you, and typing that code back in signs you in. Your device then holds a sign-in token so it does not have to ask again for a while.

The email itself is sent by Resend on the app's behalf. They handle the delivery of that message, which means they see your address and the code going out.

When the app crashes

If the app hits a fault it cannot carry on from, it shows a screen saying so and sends a report to Sentry, a service that collects crashes so that broken things get fixed. What goes in the report is the error message, the place in the code, and the version of the app.

Player names, group names, ratings, schedules, email addresses, which screen you were on and what you tapped are not in it. Before a report is sent, the app strips out any name it holds on that device, in case one has found its way into the error text. Your IP address is not sent and Sentry is set not to record one.

Counting visits

Vercel, the company that hosts the app, counts page views for it. It is a count, not a profile: no cookies, no advertising identifier, and nothing that follows you to any other website. It records the address of the page, the country, and the general type of device and browser. It cannot tell one visitor apart from another, and it is never joined up with an account, a group, or a player.

Donating

The Donate button opens Ko-fi, which is a separate website run by another company. Anything you do there is covered by Ko-fi's own privacy policy, not this one. Your card details are handled by them and are never seen by this app or by me. If you donate, I see what Ko-fi shows me, which is typically a name or nickname, the amount, and any message you write.

Suggesting a feature, or reporting a bug

Those two buttons send me a message when you press Send, and nothing before then. The message goes to me by email, sent by Resend, the same company that sends the sign-in codes. A bug report has some details about the app attached, such as the version, which screen you were on, and how many groups and players you have as numbers. It does not attach any names. The panel lists exactly what is attached before you send it, and closing the panel sends nothing.

The panel asks for your email address so that I can reply, and that field is optional. Leave it blank and I have no way to write back and no record of who sent it. Fill it in, and it travels with the message and is used for nothing else. If you are signed in it starts on the address you signed in with, which you are free to change or clear.

Cookies

The app sets no cookies, for tracking or for anything else. It does use your browser's own storage to keep your groups on your device, and to keep you signed in if you have an account. That storage is readable only by this app in your browser, and is not sent to anyone. Clearing your browsing data clears it.

Where the data is kept, and for how long

The database holding accounts sits in Canada. The other companies above are in the United States and the United Kingdom, so information that reaches them, such as your email address going to Resend so it can send a code, is handled there.

There is no schedule that deletes an account for you. Your groups and players stay until you change them or delete them, which is what you want from a list of your regular players. Crash reports are kept for thirty days and then go automatically.

Deleting a group or a player leaves a marker on the server saying it went, which is how your other devices know to remove it as well. The marker keeps the name it had, and it stays until you delete the account. Nothing marked that way is shown anywhere in the app, and it is in your downloaded copy so that you can see it is there.

Backups of the database are kept so that an accident does not lose everyone's data. A deleted account is out of the live database immediately, and out of the backups within a month as the old ones are replaced.

Your rights, and the two buttons that do the work

Both of the things people normally have to write in and ask for are buttons in the app. Open the menu, choose My Account, and sign in if you are not already.

  • Download My Data saves a file holding everything the account has on the server. It is the complete answer, in the database's own words, with an explanation of those words written inside the file.
  • Delete Account ends the account and everything saved with it, at once and for good. It asks you to type the word DELETE first, because it cannot be undone. It offers you the download on the same screen. It leaves the groups and players on your device alone, so the app carries on working exactly as it does for the many people who never made an account.

If you never made an account, there is nothing to ask for. Everything you have typed is on your device, and clearing your browsing data ends it.

You can also ask me to correct something, ask what is held, or object to any of it, at jeff@pbroundrobin.com. I will answer within thirty days. In Canada, if you are not satisfied with my answer you can complain to the Office of the Privacy Commissioner of Canada. In the UK or the EU, you can complain to your national data protection authority.

If you are in the UK or the EU

The reason the app is allowed to hold what it holds is straightforward. Your email address, groups, players and settings are held to provide the account you asked for. Crash reports and visit counts are held because a working app has to be able to find out that it is broken and roughly how many people it broke for, which is a legitimate interest, and both are cut back so they say nothing about you personally. There is no advertising and no profiling here to consent to.

Canada, where the database sits, is recognised by the European Commission as providing adequate protection for personal data. Transfers to the companies listed above that are in the United States rely on their standard contractual terms.

Children

The app is built for whoever organises a game, not for children, and it is not aimed at them. It never asks anyone's age. If a junior's name is on a player list, it is there because the organiser typed it, and the organiser can remove it at any time.
